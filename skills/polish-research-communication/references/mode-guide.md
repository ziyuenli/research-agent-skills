# Mode Guide

## Manuscript

- Match the target journal's rhetorical emphasis without imitating ornamental style.
- Lead with the public-health or scientific result when the venue is result-oriented.
- Separate what was measured from what is inferred.
- Align spatial, temporal, and population scales before comparing studies.
- Do not compress a limitation into a misleading claim of disagreement.

## Mathematical formulation

- Use one symbol for one concept.
- Define the spatial support and acquisition-pair set explicitly.
- Check whether a cyclic sum is exactly zero, an integer multiple of \(2\pi\), or zero only after wrapping.
- Distinguish node-balance conservation from curl-free or potential-flow constraints.
- State orientation and sign conventions for graph quantities.

## Slides

- Build a causal transition: problem → limitation → method → evidence → implication.
- Give each slide one dominant message.
- Treat secondary plot elements as support, not competing headlines.
- Distinguish apparent deformation from verified ground motion.
- End a findings sequence with the unresolved challenge that motivates future work.

## Research email

- Put the purpose in the first paragraph.
- Provide only enough background to establish fit or context.
- Make the requested decision or next step explicit.
- Avoid repeated thanks, exaggerated deference, and claims that presume the recipient's agreement.
- Use “inquiry” rather than “application” when no formal position or process exists.

## CV and proposal

- Preserve the user's actual role: PI, co-investigator, contributor, or applicant.
- Use one line when requested; do not add unexplained identifiers.
- In a proposal, connect background to the unmet need before introducing prediction or application.
- Separate objective, method, anticipated result, and program fit.
